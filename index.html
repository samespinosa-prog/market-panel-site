Signs of trouble

4 of 14 raised
  • Prices are near the most expensive they have ever been, measured against ten years of company earnings.
  • Borrowed money in the market is at an extreme — and our exit plan does not currently watch it at all.
  • Still expensive even after allowing for today's interest rates.
  • 1966 is the warning. Back then lenders were even calmer than now and profits were growing well. What followed was the worst decade for shares on record. Calm lenders describe today's mood; they do not promise tomorrow.

Signs things are fine

+13.6% typical year after
  • Up 13.6% typically — when the market has been at a record high and this expensive before, the next year was up 71% of the time, and by more than the 8.9% typical of record highs generally. Expensive has not meant the next year goes badly.
  • Profits are growing fast — at 5.3% a year after inflation, the strongest ten-year stretch since records began in 1891. Whatever else is true, the earnings underneath these prices are real. This is not the dot-com bubble.
  • Some of the "expensive" is an illusion — because profits grew so fast, the ten-year average they are measured against lags behind. Correct for it and the number is 35.4 rather than 40.0. Still high, but not the headline figure.
  • Lenders are relaxed — the single most reliable measure here is as calm as it has been at any point since 1927.
  • Most years are fine — 89 out of every 100 years do not begin a crash at all.

Neither column is a verdict. The count on the left has never been tested as a rule, and the figures on the right come from only a handful of separate periods — one of which ran straight into a 49% fall. Both sides are thin evidence pointing in opposite directions. That is honestly where this sits.

What "track record" means

Each warning sign was checked against the eleven crashes since 1928. The score is the chance it looked worse before a crash than during a calm stretch. 0.50 means it was no more useful than tossing a coin. The best one here reaches 0.61.

One measure — how anxious the news sounds — was included deliberately as a dud. It scored 0.49, which is what a useless measure should score. That it landed there is our check that the scoring is honest.

Why none of this says "sell"

Crashes are rare — about eleven in the last ninety-eight years. When something is that rare, even a very good warning sign raises roughly one false alarm for every real one. There is no way around that; it is just arithmetic.

So this is a dial, not a switch. It tells you whether to lean, and by how much. It cannot tell you to get out.

Holding both at once

Prices and borrowing really are near records. Profit growth really is the strongest on record, and the most reliable measure here really is calm. Both are true at the same time, and neither cancels the other.

Where that leaves us hasn't changed: these measures say a lot about the next ten years and almost nothing about the next one. So stay invested, because nobody can time the turn — and decide the exit rules now, while everyone is calm, because they will have to work on the worst day.
